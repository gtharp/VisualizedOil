Texas: the RRC.

Railroad Commission of Texas — the agency that decides where wells go in Texas. Here is what it holds, what it doesn't, and the one document worth your afternoon.

The agency

Where it came from

Created in 1891 to regulate railroad rates. Picked up petroleum pipelines as common carriers in 1917, and full oil and gas conservation jurisdiction in March 1919. Its rail functions were handed to the Texas Department of Transportation in 2005 — the name is all that's left of them.

If you only do one thing

The document to pull

Texas · start here

The drilling permit (Form W-1) and its plat

The permit is what an operator has to file before a bit turns, and the plat stapled to it is a survey drawing showing exactly where the wellbore goes and what acreage the well is dedicated to. It is the closest thing you will get to a free picture of your unit.

  1. Open the RRC Public GIS Viewer and navigate to your survey, abstract, or section — or search by operator name if you know who is drilling.
  2. Click the well to get its API number and lease number. Write both down; every other RRC search wants one of them.
  3. Pull the W-1 drilling permit and open the attached plat. Check the acreage and the survey calls against your own deed.
  4. Pull the P-16 Acreage Designation, which states the acreage assigned to the well — the denominator in your decimal.
  5. Finish with the PR production query to see what the lease has actually produced, month by month.

Texas is the anchor for this whole section, and it is genuinely one of the most generous public records systems in the country. Everything here is free, no account, no subscription.

Worth knowing

What else this agency publishes

Public GIS ViewerEvery permitted and producing well in Texas on a map, with survey and abstract lines.
Completion reports (W-2 oil / G-1 gas)Filed after the well is finished: total depth, perforated interval, initial test.
Form P-4Names the operator and the purchaser — in other words, who is supposed to be paying you.
Rule 37 & 38 exception docketsIf a well sits closer to a lease line or another well than the rules allow, there is a hearing — and a file.

Named here rather than linked directly, because deep links into state records systems break more often than the systems do. Search the name from www.rrc.texas.gov.

Before you start

Two things to have in hand

Your legal description

Every search on Texas's system wants to know where, and none of them accept a street address as the real answer. Get the section, township and range — or the survey and abstract — off your deed or an old check stub before you open the website. Without it you are guessing.

The API number, once you find it

Every Texas well number begins 42. Once you have a well's full API number, write it down: it is the key that works in the state system, in FracFocus, and in every commercial database — and it never changes. Take one apart if you have not seen the structure before.

The thing to remember

The RRC holds the well and the unit. It does not hold your ownership. Deeds, leases, probates and the chain of title live with the county clerk in the county where the minerals are — and no state oil and gas agency in the country can tell you what you own.
